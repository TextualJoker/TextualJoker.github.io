The opportunity

Everyone is trying. Almost nobody has scaled it.

Two things are true at once, and the distance between them is the entire argument for doing this deliberately.

Where the industry actually is
MeasureFigureSource
Software professionals using AI at work90%DORA 2025 — up 14 points year on year

Those capabilities actually running in production~37%Capgemini 2025–26

Adopted the tools faster than they wrote any policy80%CTO survey 2026

Nine in ten are trying. Fewer than one in six have scaled it.

That gap is not a capability problem — the models are the same ones everybody has. It is a process and evidence problem, which is why the investment goes into the delivery process rather than the tooling.

The last row is the sharpest: four in five organisations adopted the tools before they wrote the policy. Starting from nothing means doing it the other way round, and that advantage expires roughly eighteen months from the first commit.

The amplifier thesis, and why it is not a slogan

DORA's 2025 framing is that AI amplifies an organisation's existing strengths and weaknesses rather than adding capability independently. Good process gets faster; bad process gets faster at producing problems.

What makes that more than a nice phrase is the list underneath it. DORA identified seven capabilities that determine whether AI accelerates outcomes or accelerates chaos:

  • A clear, communicated position on what AI may be used for
  • Healthy data ecosystems
  • Internal data an agent can actually reach
  • Strong version control practices
  • Working in small batches
  • User-centric focus
  • Quality internal platforms
The observation worth repeating in a room

Five of the seven are ordinary engineering hygiene. Nothing about model choice, prompt technique or tool selection appears anywhere on the list.

That is what "amplifier, not lever" means in practice — and it is why the quality strategy and the AI strategy turn out to be the same strategy.

Reconciling them

METR studied experts working on code they already knew intimately. In that setting the developer's mental model is already better than anything an agent can reconstruct, so prompting and reviewing is pure overhead. Microsoft studied a broad population across unfamiliar surfaces, a year later, with better tooling.

The Microsoft data supports that reading from the inside. The effect was dose-responsive — from about +15% at three days a week to +50% at five or more. Junior engineers and long-tenured staff saw the largest lifts. And senior individual contributors, the population most like METR's subjects, adopted at high rates but retained at close to zero. They tried it and stopped.

What follows for a codebase being built from scratch

With no legacy mental models to compete with, we sit firmly in the population where gains appear. Two caveats travel with that, and both matter more than the headline.

Our most senior people may genuinely be slower on the work they know best. Do not mandate uniform usage — it costs credibility with exactly the people whose support matters most.

Microsoft measured merged pull requests. The next section measures what is inside them. Optimising the first without watching the second is how you buy throughput with maintainability.

The finding that should change how you argue for this

Developers in the METR trial believed they were 20% faster while being measured 19% slower. The paper also records a 24% forecast made before the study.

Never justify this programme on developer sentiment. Self-reported productivity was measured as inverted, and it is the easiest evidence to gather and the least worth having.

Security

Two studies, two different numbers, and the gap between them is methodological rather than contradictory.

  • Roughly one in four. 534 samples across six frontier models tested against the standard web risk list; about 25% contained a confirmed vulnerability. Best model 19.1%, worst 29.2%. Server-side request forgery was the single most common class — models routinely generate outbound calls that accept a caller-supplied address without validating it.
  • Roughly one in two. A separate study found 45% of samples failed a security test — a looser measure. Log injection failed 88% of the time, cross-site scripting 86%.
  • Why they differ: "confirmed vulnerability" is a much higher bar than "failed a test". The honest reading is a floor of one in four and a plausible ceiling near one in two.

Two supporting figures: 56% of organisations report a security incident at least partly attributable to AI-generated code, and time-to-detect for an AI-introduced vulnerability runs about 42 days longer than for human-written code — because existing tooling was not designed for the patterns AI produces.

How to use these numbers

Lead with 25%. A claim that survives scrutiny is worth more than a bigger one that invites an argument about methodology — and the conclusion is identical at either figure: too high to leave to human review alone.

It is also why the security reviewer is a separate agent rather than an instruction to the code reviewer, and why it is weighted toward the classes that actually appear rather than spreading attention evenly.

The constraint

Where the constraint moves

This is the most decision-relevant evidence in the document. Almost every design choice descends from it.

  • Telemetry from more than ten thousand developers: 98% more pull requests opened, 91% longer review times, and flat delivery metrics. Twice the work going in, the same value coming out.
  • Agent-opened pull requests wait 5.3× longer to be picked up than unassisted ones. AI-assisted ones wait 2.47× longer.
  • Feature-branch throughput rose 59% year on year while throughput on the main branch actually fell. Main-branch success rates dropped to 70.8%.
  • Output per engineer rose roughly 60% between 2025 and 2026, while a reviewer still handles the same number of changes they always did.

Generation is nearly free. Review is not.

The first bullet is the clearest single statement of the problem: twice the input, no change in output. The extra volume landed entirely on the one part of the process that did not get faster — a person reading it.

So the design test for anything proposed is: does this make review cheaper, or does it add to the queue? Risk lanes, size caps, machine review before any human sees a change, and automated rollback all exist for that one reason.

Anything that raises generation without raising review throughput makes the organisation slower, not faster.

The direct consequence for how we work

This is the evidence against running several agent sessions per developer in parallel. It is a real technique and it works — but it multiplies input to the stage that is already the constraint. Fix review throughput first, then scale generation into the headroom you created. Doing it the other way round is how the 98% number happens.

Agent design

How agents actually fail

There is a taxonomy for this, built from real execution traces, and it argues against the most popular way of organising agents.

Berkeley researchers analysed more than 1,600 annotated execution traces across seven multi-agent frameworks, producing fourteen failure modes in three categories. Inter-annotator agreement was 0.88, which is high.

Failures by category, and the mode people design against
CategoryShareWhat it means
Specification and system design41.8%The largest by a wide margin. The task was described wrongly, or the system around it was built wrongly. Includes step repetition, missing termination conditions, and disobeying the task.
Inter-agent misalignment36.9%Agents losing information between handoffs — reasoning that doesn't match the action taken, derailment, failure to ask for clarification.
Task verification21.3%Incorrect verification, incomplete verification, stopping early.
"Disobey role specification"0.5%The rarest mode in the entire taxonomy — and the one a role-based agent team is built to prevent.

The authors also tested interventions. Adding high-level task verification improved success by +15.6%; adjusting roles and workflow gained +9.4%.

The persona evidence

A systematic study across four model families, 162 roles and 2,410 factual questions found that adding personas to system prompts produced no improvement, and often a small negative effect, against a control with no persona. A 2026 follow-up refined rather than overturned this: role prompting systematically increases apparent expertise depth while reducing clarity.

What the vendor's own research says

  • Multi-agent architectures excel at breadth-first problems that split into independent parallel strands, and are less effective for tightly interdependent tasks — coding is named specifically.
  • Multi-agent systems consume roughly 15× the tokens of standard interactions.
  • Token usage alone explains about 80% of performance variance — more than prompt engineering or architecture.

Reading these together

Giving agents job titles optimises a failure mode representing 0.5% of observed failures, at roughly 15× the token cost, on a task type the model vendor's own research says multi-agent handles poorly — while the categories that actually dominate go unaddressed.

What is real is the boundary, not the badge: separate context, restricted tools, independence from the work being reviewed, and cost tier. Every agent should be justified by one of those four. If you cannot name which, you are writing a costume.

Where bounded loops come in

The most-repeated cautionary story in this field — an agent pair that looped for eleven days and produced a $47,000 bill — has no traceable primary source. Treat it as an illustration and never as evidence.

The measured version makes the point better: formal resource limits on agent loops produced a 90% reduction in token consumption and 525× lower run-to-run variance. Variance is the real finding — an unbounded loop is not merely expensive, it is unbudgetable.

The programme killer

What kills these programmes

The most instructive failure in the public record is not a team that used agents badly. It is a team that used them well and lost the budget argument anyway.

Microsoft introduced Claude Code to engineers in December 2025. Adoption reached 84–95% of the cohort by April. Its own researchers measured a 24% increase in merged pull requests that did not decay over four months. In June 2026 it was cancelled for roughly 5,000 engineers in one division, who were moved to a cheaper tool.

The mechanism is easy to repeat. The pilot ran on flat seat licensing, which made consumption invisible during exactly the months when usage habits were forming. When billing moved to consumption, the real figure — $500 to $2,000 per engineer per month — arrived all at once, against a budget set when nobody knew what the number would be.

They had the money. They lost the argument.

Four controls follow directly, and all four are free if done at the start.

Usage-based billing from day one — never pilot on a flat rate and convert later; you want the cost signal present while habits form. Per-user caps, not per-team — team caps punish your highest-value users by averaging them with light ones. Spend visible to the engineer generating it — the cheapest cost control is a developer who can see their own number. And track cost per merged change, not per seat, because that is the question a finance director actually asks and the one Microsoft could not answer in its own defence.

How to use this table

Everything marked verified was checked and is exact. Lead with those — they carry the whole argument on their own.

The rest are directionally sound but should not be quoted to the decimal in a room where someone might check. A claim you can defend beats a bigger claim you cannot, and none of the recommendations change if you drop every unverified row.

One caveat on the whole set

This field moves fast enough that the METR result is already labelled historical by the people who produced it, and the tooling generation it tested is two generations old. Treat the direction as durable and the magnitudes as dated. The structural findings — where the constraint sits, what happens to duplication, where agent failures originate — have held across every study since.
